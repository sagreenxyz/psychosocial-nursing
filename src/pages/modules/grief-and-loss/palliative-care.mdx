## Palliative Care vs. Hospice Care

| Concept | Definition |
|---------|-----------|
| **Palliative care** | Specialized care focused on relief from symptoms, pain, and stress of serious illness — provided *alongside* curative treatment, at any stage of illness |
| **Hospice care** | A philosophy and program of care for patients in the last 6 months of life who have chosen to forgo curative treatment and focus on comfort |

> A common misconception is that palliative care means "giving up." In fact, early palliative care improves quality of life *and* has been shown to extend survival in some studies (Temel et al., 2010).

## Psychosocial Needs at the End of Life

Beyond physical symptom management, patients facing death have profound **psychosocial and existential needs**:

- **Being known as a whole person** — not reduced to a disease or prognosis
- **Maintaining relationships** — Time with family and loved ones
- **Dignity and autonomy** — Control over remaining decisions
- **Meaning and legacy** — Life review, leaving something behind
- **Spiritual peace** — Resolution of existential questions; forgiveness
- **Freedom from fear** — Reassurance about the dying process

## The COMFORT Communication Framework

| Letter | Component | Clinical Application |
|--------|-----------|---------------------|
| **C** | **Communicate** | Use clear, compassionate language; avoid jargon |
| **O** | **Orientation** | Orient to patient/family goals, values, and concerns |
| **M** | **Mindful presence** | Be fully present; resist the urge to "fix" |
| **F** | **Family engagement** | Include family as unit of care |
| **O** | **Openings** | Follow the patient's lead; listen for openings to discuss fears |
| **R** | **Relating** | Connect as a human, not just a clinician |
| **T** | **Team** | Coordinate with interdisciplinary team (chaplain, social work, palliative care) |

## Breaking Bad News: The SPIKES Framework

- **S** — Setting (private space, appropriate attendees)
- **P** — Perception (what does the patient know/expect?)
- **I** — Invitation (does the patient want information now?)
- **K** — Knowledge (share information in plain language)
- **E** — Emotion/Empathy (acknowledge and respond to emotional reaction)
- **S** — Strategy and Summary (next steps, support offered)

## Supporting Families

Families of dying patients often carry enormous burdens:
- **Caregiver burden** — Physical and emotional exhaustion
- **Anticipatory grief** — Grieving before death occurs
- **Decision-making pressure** — Navigating advance directives, goals of care

**Nursing interventions for families:**
- Include family in care planning conversations (with patient consent)
- Provide clear, honest information about what dying typically looks like
- Connect families with social work, chaplaincy, and bereavement programs
- Ensure all family members have opportunity to say goodbye if desired

---

## Self-Care for Nurses

Nurses who care for dying patients regularly are at elevated risk for **compassion fatigue** and **moral distress**. Protective strategies include:

- **Meaning-making** — Recognize the privilege and meaning of being present at life's end
- **Ritual** — Some nurses develop personal rituals to honor patients who have died
- **Peer support and debriefs** — Processing difficult deaths with colleagues
- **Professional support** — Employee assistance programs, counseling

---

## Key Takeaways

- Palliative care is appropriate at any stage of serious illness — not only at the end of life
- Dying patients have profound psychosocial and existential needs alongside physical ones
- Skilled communication (COMFORT, SPIKES) is as important as technical care at end of life
- Nurses must attend to their own grief and moral distress from caring for dying patients
