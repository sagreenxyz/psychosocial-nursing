## Why Risk Assessment Matters

Suicide is a leading cause of death globally — and nurses across all settings encounter patients at elevated risk. Comprehensive, systematic risk assessment is a core nursing competency that saves lives.

> **Important:** Risk assessment is not a one-time event. It should be conducted regularly, especially when there is a change in the patient's condition, new stressors, or transitions in care.

---

## Key Definitions

- **Suicidal ideation:** Thoughts of killing oneself, ranging from passive ("I wish I were dead") to active with specific plan and intent
- **Suicide attempt:** A self-directed, potentially injurious behavior with intent to die
- **Non-suicidal self-injury (NSSI):** Deliberate self-harm *without* suicidal intent (e.g., cutting to manage emotional pain)
- **Protective factors:** Resources, beliefs, relationships, and strengths that reduce suicide risk

---

## Risk Factors for Suicide

### Psychiatric Risk Factors
- Depression (especially with hopelessness)
- Bipolar disorder (highest risk during mixed episodes)
- Schizophrenia (especially with command hallucinations)
- Substance use disorders (significantly amplify risk)
- **Previous suicide attempts (strongest single predictor of future attempts)**
- PTSD, borderline personality disorder

### Psychosocial Risk Factors
- Recent significant loss (job, relationship, loved one)
- Social isolation and loneliness
- History of childhood trauma or abuse
- Financial crisis or legal problems

---

## Protective Factors

| Category | Examples |
|----------|---------|
| **Individual** | Reasons for living, problem-solving skills, future orientation |
| **Social** | Strong family connections, social support, sense of belonging |
| **Clinical** | Current engagement in treatment, willingness to seek help |
| **Environmental** | Means restriction (no access to firearms, medications locked) |

## Structured Risk Assessment: The C-SSRS

The **Columbia Suicide Severity Rating Scale (C-SSRS)** is a validated, evidence-based tool used across healthcare settings.

### Ideation Assessment (ask in order):
1. "Have you wished you were dead or wished you could go to sleep and not wake up?"
2. "Have you had any thoughts of killing yourself?"
3. *(If yes)* "Have you been thinking about how you might do this?"
4. "Have you had any intention of acting on these thoughts?"
5. "Have you started to work out or worked out the details of how to kill yourself? Do you intend to carry out this plan?"

> A **positive response to items 3–5** constitutes "active suicidal ideation with plan and/or intent" — a psychiatric emergency requiring immediate intervention.

---

## Risk Stratification

| Level | Characteristics | Response |
|-------|----------------|---------|
| **Low** | Passive ideation only, no plan, strong protective factors | Safety planning, monitoring, outpatient follow-up |
| **Moderate** | Ideation with some plan, limited intent | Intensive outpatient or partial hospitalization |
| **High** | Ideation with plan and intent, limited protective factors | Psychiatric hospitalization; 1:1 supervision |
| **Imminent** | Intent to act, specific plan, no reason to stay safe | Emergency intervention; stat psychiatric consultation |

---

## Safety Planning

A **collaborative safety plan** (not a "no-suicide contract") is an evidence-based intervention. Key components:

1. **Warning signs** — What thoughts, feelings, or behaviors signal escalating risk?
2. **Internal coping strategies** — What can the patient do alone to distract or self-soothe?
3. **Social support** — Who can they talk to? (List names and numbers)
4. **Professional resources** — Clinician contact, crisis line (988), emergency services
5. **Means restriction** — Remove or restrict access to lethal means

---

## Key Takeaways

- Risk assessment for suicide should be structured, systematic, and documented thoroughly
- The C-SSRS provides a validated, evidence-based framework for stratifying risk
- Safety planning is a collaborative, evidence-based intervention — not a "no-harm contract"
- Protective factors are as clinically important as risk factors
