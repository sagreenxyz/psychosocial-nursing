## What Is De-escalation?

**De-escalation** is a set of verbal and non-verbal communication strategies used to reduce the intensity of a patient's agitated, distressed, or potentially threatening behavior. It is a first-line, evidence-based alternative to physical restraint or involuntary medication.

> De-escalation does not mean permitting unsafe behavior — it means addressing the emotional and situational drivers of agitation before behavior becomes dangerous.

---

## Recognizing Escalation: The STAMP Mnemonic

| Letter | Sign |
|--------|------|
| **S** | Staring — Intense, fixed gaze |
| **T** | Tone — Voice becoming louder, higher-pitched |
| **A** | Anxiety — Visible restlessness, agitation, rapid breathing |
| **M** | Mumbling — Under-breath verbalization, muttering |
| **P** | Pacing — Repetitive movement, inability to stay still |

## Core De-escalation Principles

### 1. Ensure Your Own Safety First
- Maintain a safe distance (at least arm's length)
- Position yourself near an exit — never allow yourself to be cornered
- A slight offset position is safer and less confrontational than standing directly in front

### 2. Use a Calm, Confident Tone
Your voice is your primary tool. Speak slowly, softly, and clearly.

### 3. Validate Feelings Without Agreeing with Behavior
> "I can see you're incredibly frustrated right now, and I want to understand what's happening for you."

### 4. Offer Choices
Agitation often stems from feeling powerless. Offering genuine choices restores autonomy:
> "Would you prefer we talk here or find a quieter room?"

### 5. Listen Actively
- Do not argue or debate
- Do not tell the patient they are wrong
- Reflect back what you hear: "It sounds like you feel like no one has been listening to you."

### 6. Avoid Triggers
- Don't invade personal space
- Don't touch without permission
- Don't issue ultimatums or threats
- Don't argue with delusions directly
- Don't have multiple staff speaking at once

### 7. Set Limits Clearly and Calmly
> "I need you to step back. I want to keep talking with you and I want both of us to be safe."

## The LOWLINE Model of De-escalation

| Element | Description |
|---------|-------------|
| **L** — Listen | Attend fully to what the patient is saying |
| **O** — Open body language | Non-threatening posture |
| **W** — Watch | Monitor escalation signals continuously |
| **L** — Limit setting | Clear, calm limits when needed |
| **I** — Identify needs | What does the patient actually need right now? |
| **N** — Non-judgmental | Avoid blaming or labeling |
| **E** — Empathy | Consistent empathic presence |

## When De-escalation Is Not Enough

De-escalation has limits. More restrictive interventions may be required when:
- The patient is actively harming themselves or others
- Verbal communication is impossible (acute psychosis, severe intoxication)
- There is an imminent threat to safety despite multiple attempts

**Escalation of intervention (most restrictive last):**
1. Verbal de-escalation
2. Voluntary medication (oral anxiolytic or antipsychotic)
3. Show of force (staff presence)
4. Involuntary medication (IM administration)
5. Physical restraint (last resort, requires clinical justification and monitoring)

---

## Key Takeaways

- De-escalation is a first-line, evidence-based alternative to physical restraint
- Early recognition of escalation (STAMP) enables proactive intervention
- Core techniques include calm tone, validation, active listening, offering choices, and clear limit-setting
- Nurses must know when to escalate to more restrictive interventions
