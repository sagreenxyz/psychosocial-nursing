## What Is Active Listening?

**Active listening** is a deliberate, fully engaged process of receiving, understanding, and responding to a speaker. It goes far beyond hearing words — it involves attending to tone, body language, emotional content, and what is *not* said.

In nursing, active listening communicates respect and genuine interest. It helps patients feel heard, reduces distress, encourages disclosure, and strengthens the therapeutic relationship.

> "The most basic of all human needs is the need to understand and be understood. The best way to understand people is to listen to them." — *Ralph Nichols*

## The SOLER Framework

Gerard Egan's **SOLER** framework provides a memorable guide to attentive physical presence:

| Letter | Behavior |
|--------|----------|
| **S** | **Sit squarely** — Face the patient, not at an angle |
| **O** | **Open posture** — Avoid crossed arms or legs |
| **L** | **Lean in** — Slightly toward the patient to show interest |
| **E** | **Eye contact** — Maintain appropriate, culturally sensitive eye contact |
| **R** | **Relax** — Avoid fidgeting; convey calm and presence |

> **Cultural note:** Eye contact norms vary significantly across cultures. Always adapt your approach to the individual patient's cultural background.

## Active Listening Techniques

### Verbal Techniques

**1. Minimal encouragers**
Brief verbal cues that signal attention without interrupting:
- "Yes..." / "I see..." / "Mm-hmm..." / "Go on..."

**2. Paraphrasing**
Restating the content of what the patient said in your own words:
> Patient: "I've been trying to eat better, but I just don't have the energy to cook anymore."
> Nurse: "So you're motivated to improve your diet, but fatigue is making it really hard."

**3. Reflecting feelings**
Naming the emotion embedded in the patient's words:
> Patient: *(speaking quickly, arms crossed)* "My family keeps saying I need to try harder."
> Nurse: "It sounds like that feels unfair to you."

**4. Clarifying questions**
> "When you say you 'can't cope,' can you help me understand what that's like for you?"

**5. Summarizing**
> "So to make sure I understand — you've been feeling low for about three weeks, sleeping poorly, and you're concerned about how this is affecting your work. Is that right?"

## What Blocks Active Listening?

- **Planning your next response** while the patient is still talking
- **Environmental distractions** — phones, alarms, interruptions
- **Making assumptions** based on the chart or previous encounters
- **Emotional reactions** to content (disgust, distress, over-identification)
- **Time pressure** — rushing through interactions

## Practice Scenario

**Read the following patient statement and formulate an active listening response:**

> "I've been in and out of the hospital for two years now. My kids are getting tired of driving me. My husband passed last year. I just wonder sometimes if any of this is worth it."

**Ineffective response:**
> "I'm sure your kids don't mind. Let's focus on getting you better!"

**Active listening response:**
> *(Pauses, maintains eye contact, leans slightly forward)*
> "It sounds like you've been carrying a lot — the illness, the losses, feeling like a burden to your family. And when you say you wonder if 'this is worth it,' can you tell me more about what you mean by that?"

*Note: The second response opens the door to a potential safety assessment while validating the patient's profound exhaustion.*

---

## Key Takeaways

- Active listening is a deliberate skill requiring full attention to verbal and non-verbal content
- The SOLER framework guides physical positioning for attentive listening
- Specific techniques — minimal encouragers, paraphrasing, reflecting, clarifying, summarizing — demonstrate active engagement
- Awareness of personal barriers to listening is essential for professional growth
